Essays

Most writing tasks in the final exams are going to be either **expository** essays or **argumentative** essays. In the case of an expository essay, the arguments should be explicative and objective. While an argumentative one should have reasons related to ‘Why do we agree or disagree with the text?’ question.

* **The "4-Paragraph" Rule**: While the 4-paragraph structure (Introduction, Body 1, Body 2, Conclusion) is a classic academic "template," an essay can actually have more paragraphs depending on the complexity of the subject.
* **The Purpose**: It’s not just an "opinion"; it's a formal piece of writing where you use claims, citations, and references to support your ideas.
* **The Language**: In this course, you are expected to use specific grammatical tools like nominalization, complex noun phrases, and cohesive devices (connectors) to link your ideas clearly.

# Formulating a sentence

[Verb] + [Object] + [Conditions] + [Finality]

**Example 1:** On Frontend Development

[Verb]: Implement[Object]: a responsive navigation bar

[Conditions]: using Tailwind CSS and Angular components

[Finality]: to improve the user experience on mobile devices.

Full sentence: "Implement a responsive navigation bar using Tailwind CSS and Angular components to improve the user experience on mobile devices."

**Example 2**: On Database Management

[Verb]: Optimize

[Object]: SQL queries

[Conditions]: through the use of proper indexing and MikroORM configurations

[Finality]: in order to reduce server response time.

Full sentence: "Optimize SQL queries through the use of proper indexing and MikroORM configurations in order to reduce server response time."

**Example 3**: On Project Methodology

[Object]: in collaborative teams

[Conditions]: by using version control systems like Git

[Finality]: so as to maintain a clean and organized codebase.Full sentence: "Participate in collaborative teams by using version control systems like Git so as to maintain a clean and organized codebase."

# Writing an expository essay

In general all paragraphs should begin with a topic sentences that summarizes what the paragraph is about.

## How to write an introductory paragraph

In academic English, an introduction usually follows a "funnel" shape: it starts broad and narrows down to your specific point.

* **The Hook (General Context)**: Start with a broad statement about the topic. According to your syllabus, this should relate to the "socio-historical context" of the technology. It’s a good idea to paraphrase the title or use a question to start this paragraph
* **The Bridge (Narrowing Down)**: Connect that broad idea to the specific area you will discuss. This is where you might mention "current trends or advancements".
* **The Thesis Statement (The Formula)**: This is the most important sentence. It tells the reader exactly what the essay is about. This is where you use the [Verb] + [Object] + [Purpose] structure.

In the case of an **argumentative** essay, this should be the paragraph in which you give your opinion.

## How to write arguments

Right after the introduction comes the arguments’ paragraph. It is fundamental to use connectors between the sentences.

## How to write conclusión paragraph

Usually what comes after the arguments is the **conclusion**. This paragraph is intended to sum up what was said in the essay and bring an ending to it. It usually starts with: ‘To sum up’, ‘In conclusion’, ‘At last’, …

The structure of an for and against essay is, in some parts, similar to what was explained in the ‘Writing an expository essay part’. An argumentative essay should also have 4 paragraphs (more or less). Having the same three parts any essay would:

1. an **introduction** in which you present the topic, making a general remark about it without giving your opinion
2. a **main body** in which you present the points for and the points against, in separate paragraphs, supporting your arguments with justifications/examples
3. a **conclusion** which includes your opinion or a balanced summary of the topic.

There are some important things to consider:

* You must not include opinion words (I believe, I think, etc.) in the introduction or the main body. Opinion words can only be used in the final paragraph, where you may state your opinion on the topic.
* Begin each paragraph with a topic sentence which summarizes what the paragraph is about.
* Write well-developed paragraphs in which the points you present are supported with justification (i.e. reasons or examples):

# Small tips

## Examples

When giving examples, avoid using “For example”, use instead “E.G.:” (exempli gratia)

## Ways to introduce a topic

**A rhetorical question:** "Is it true that [Topic] will change the way we live?"

**Historical context:** "A hundred years ago, the only way to [Action] was by..."

**Modern contrast:** "Nowadays, however, it is possible to [Action]..."

**General remark:** "[Topic] is one of the most important aspects of our lives today."

**Objective presentation:** "The inclusion of [Topic] in the field of engineering is a subject of growing interest."

## Ways to start the advantages paragraph

"The main/greatest advantage of [Topic] is..."

"One very convincing argument in favour of [Topic] is..."

"It is widely argued/suggested that [Topic] offers several benefits."

"An additional major advantage of [Topic] involves..."

"Advocates of [Topic] support the view that..."

## Ways to start the disadvantage paragraph

"On the other hand, [Topic] does have its disadvantages."

"However, there are many arguments against using [Topic]."

"A further common criticism of [Topic] is..."

"Opponents of [Topic] argue that it presents several drawbacks."

"Another negative aspect of [Topic] is the potential for..."

## Ways to Start the Conclusion Paragraph

"All things considered, I believe that..."

"Taking everything into account, it is my belief that..."

"On balance, although there are some disadvantages, I feel…”

"To sum up, it appears that the benefits of [Topic] outweigh the risks."

"In conclusion, the best course of action would be to..."

## Connecting Words (Linking Phrases)

**To add more points:** *Furthermore*, *Moreover*, *In addition*, *What is more*, *Besides*.

**To show examples:** *For instance*, *For example*, *Such as*, *Particularly*, *This is clearly shown by*.

**To show result/effect:** *Therefore*, *Consequently*, *As a result*, *Thus*.

**To express purpose:** *In order to*, *So as to*, *With the intention of*.

**To clarify:** *In other words*, *That is to say*, *Which means that*.

Debates

Debates sets up the student in the propositions team (agreeing with whatever the subject is) or the oppositions team (disagreeing on the same subject). There may be moderators whose function is to make the conversation fluid and balanced between the two teams.

# Commonly used ways of talking during a debate

## Starting as the First Speaker

If you are the one opening the discussion or the first to respond to a prompt, you need to set a formal tone.

* **"To kick things off, it is widely argued that..."**
* **"I would like to start by highlighting the fact that..."**
* **"The first point I would like to raise regarding this issue is..."**
* **"If we look at the current status of [Topic], it is clear that..."**

## Agreeing and Disagreeing (The Spectrum)

| **Level of Agreement** | **Expression** |
| --- | --- |
| **Heavily Agreeing** | “I **firmly believe** your point is correct and it cannot be denied that…” |
| **Agreeing in parts** | “I **concede validity** to your argument up to a point, but one must also consider” |
| **Minimum Agreeing** | “While **there is some truth** in what yo usay, it is also a fact that…” |
| **Disagreeing** | “I see your point, but I **oppose the view that** [Point] is the only factor” |
| **Strong Disagreement** | “I **strongly disagree** with that claim because it contradicts the idea that…” |

## Asking permission and taking turns

* **To interrupt politely:** "If I may just add a point here..."
* **To ask for a turn:** "Could I just say something regarding the previous point?"
* **To hold your turn (if you need to think):** "That is an interesting question; let me just clarify my position on..."
* **To invite the other person:** "What is your stance on this particular aspect?"

Other Useful Expressions

* **To clarify your own point:** "In other words, what I am trying to suggest is..."
* **To add an example during a speech:** "A striking example of this is the fact that..."
* **To summarize a consensus:** "It appears we have reached a balance between our viewpoints regarding..."
