Karen: Welcome to Power Talk. Today I'm joined by two engineers, Ravi Menon and Niamh Keane. Let's start with the big question: how do we avoid blackouts when we depend more and more on solar and wind? Ravi?
Rishi: Well, my view is simple. The cheapest tool we have is the customer. If a utility pays households to use less electricity at peak times, what we call demand response, we avoid building power plants that only work a few hours a year. A small payment for switching off the air conditioning for twenty minutes is a fair deal for everybody.
Moira: I like the logic, Ravi, and for factories I would agree, because one manager makes the decision. But households? Families won't change their habits for a few cents. I've seen the surveys, and most people simply can't be bothered.
Rishi: With automation they don't have to bother. The washing machine just waits for the cheap hour.
Moira: Maybe. But that brings me to my main worry, and it isn't the money. It's the data. A smart meter knows when you wake up, when you cook and when you go on holiday. For me, that is an invasion of privacy.
Rishi: Some people say so, but I don't agree. Privacy is manageable. Data can be anonymised, and strict laws exist. What really worries me is the hardware: cheap devices that break.
Moira: Hardware failure is real, but I would put cyberattacks first. Think of Ukraine in twenty fifteen: hackers switched off the power for about two hundred thousand people, in winter. Sorry, two hundred and twenty-five thousand.
Rishi: Honestly, I think that risk is overstated. Those attackers needed months of preparation, and a well-run utility can detect them.
Moira: And yet it worked. The more devices you connect, Ravi, the more doors you open. Every smart meter is a door.
Rishi: Hmm. Okay, you have a point. If millions of devices have weak passwords, one flaw is enough. So yes, I now agree it is a serious threat. Probably the most serious one.
Karen: Let's talk about the timeline. What is realistic? Niamh, start with batteries.
Moira: Let me be careful. Some people say that every house should have its own battery. Frankly, that is too expensive for most families, so I would drop that idea. What I do see is community batteries, big units serving a whole neighbourhood. There are pilot schemes now, but I'd expect them to be common within three years. No, sorry, five years. Covering every neighbourhood will take far longer.
Rishi: I agree. And dynamic pricing, where the price changes hour by hour, is already available from several utilities as we speak. What will take five years is offering it to every household.
Karen: And the full vision, the self-healing national grid?
Rishi: Parts of it exist now. A few sections of the network already heal themselves, and within five years I expect whole regions to work that way. But a whole country? That is a long-term goal. Twenty years at least. Maybe more.
Moira: Yes, I'd say the same. The regulation also needs to catch up.
Karen: A final thought from each of you?
Rishi: Technology is not the obstacle. People and money are.
Moira: And security must be designed from the start, not added later.
Karen: Thank you both.
