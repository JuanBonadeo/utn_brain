### Are driverless trucks the future of freight?

*by Marta Ellison, logistics analyst*

**A** Roughly seven out of every ten tonnes of freight in the United States travel by
road, and the people who move it are in short supply. The average long-haul driver is
now over forty-five, many are close to retirement, and the long periods away from home
make recruitment difficult. Industry associations warn that the gap between the number
of drivers and the amount of freight is likely to grow over the coming years.
Regulations add to the problem: a driver may legally drive for no more than eleven hours
a day. A human being is, quite literally, a limit on the schedule.

**B** It is not surprising, then, that engineers see an opportunity. A truck controlled
by software does not need sleep, does not get bored, and can in principle run almost
around the clock. Several start-ups have spent the last decade developing such vehicles.
The most successful have abandoned the dream of a truck that drives anywhere. They have
adopted instead a more modest scheme known as the hub-to-hub model: the vehicle drives
itself only on long, simple highway sections between two logistics centres, while humans
handle everything else.

**C** A typical journey has five stages. First, a human driver collects the load from the
factory and takes it to a transfer hub, a large logistics centre beside the highway.
There, technicians carry out a safety check of the autonomous truck and couple the
trailer to it. On the highway, the truck compares what its cameras, radar and lidar
detect with a high-resolution map, which allows it to stay in its lane and keep a safe
distance. During the whole trip, a remote operator in a control centre monitors the
vehicle's data and can help if the software becomes uncertain. Finally, at the
destination hub, a local driver takes over the trailer and covers the "last mile", the
final stretch to the customer's door.

**D** Supporters insist that safety is the strongest argument. The great majority of
road accidents are attributed to human error: fatigue, distraction, alcohol. Engineers
also design these trucks around <u>redundancy</u>. Every critical system is duplicated.
Another can take over if the first one fails. A camera blinded by the sun, for example,
is not a catastrophe, because radar and lidar keep working. If everything fails, the
vehicle is programmed to slow down and stop safely on the hard shoulder.

**E** Some problems remain unsolved. Systems that work well in the dry climate of Texas
or Arizona perform worse in heavy snow, when lane markings disappear and sensors become
covered with ice. The software also struggles with the unexpected, such as a police
officer directing traffic through roadworks or a mattress lying on the road. In such
cases the truck usually stops and waits for help from a remote operator. In addition,
every hub requires heavy investment, and the model only makes sense on long,
predictable routes, where the cost of building hubs can be recovered.

**F** Not everybody is convinced. Daniel Okoye, spokesperson for a national drivers'
association, argues: "A truck without a human on board is a danger, because no software
can cope with the unexpected as an experienced driver can." Okoye acknowledges that the
hub-to-hub model does not remove every job: someone must still drive the first and last
mile. But he fears that the new roles, such as remote operators, will be fewer and worse
paid than those that disappear.

**G** So, are driverless trucks the future of freight? Partly. On long highway routes,
hub-to-hub transport will probably become common within the next decade, because the
economics and the driver shortage push in the same direction. But the dream of a truck
that picks up a load at a factory and delivers it to a door without any human is still
far away. What will change is the job: fewer drivers on the highways, more in local
delivery and in control centres. The future of freight is neither fully human nor fully
automatic. It is shared.

### Growing up, not out: the promise of vertical farms

By 2050 the world will need to feed almost ten billion people, and farmland is already
under pressure from erosion, droughts and expanding cities. Vertical farming is not a
new idea, but cheaper LED lighting has made it commercially possible. The principle is simple: instead of using more land, use more height. In a vertical
farm, plants grow in trays stacked up to ten levels high inside a building, with LED
lights instead of sunshine and a nutrient solution instead of soil.

The advantages are considerable. Because the water is recirculated, a vertical farm can
use up to 95 per cent less than a conventional farm. Since the climate is controlled, no
pesticides are needed and harvests are possible all year round. Production is also close
to consumers: a lettuce may travel a few kilometres instead of hundreds, and losses caused by bad weather or insects are minimal. This is
attractive for a city like Singapore, which imports more than 90 per cent of its food.

However, the economics are difficult. Light is the main expense: in a typical facility,
electricity represents around 30 per cent of operating costs, slightly more than labour,
which accounts for about 25 per cent. As a result, vertical farms grow mainly leafy
greens, herbs and strawberries, which are light, quick to grow and expensive per
kilogram. Staple crops such as wheat, rice or potatoes need much more energy per
kilogram of food, so growing them indoors is, for now, unprofitable. Several well-known
vertical-farming companies have closed or declared bankruptcy in recent years, after
expanding faster than their sales.

Supporters remain optimistic. Some companies are testing robots to plant and harvest the crops, which could reduce labour costs. LEDs become more efficient every year, and a farm powered
by solar or wind energy would be far more sustainable. Most experts agree that vertical
farms will not replace fields, but they could complement them by supplying fresh
vegetables to cities.

### Nuclear power and the climate

Nuclear power plants generate electricity without burning fuel, so they emit almost no
carbon dioxide while they operate. Over its whole life cycle, a nuclear plant releases
about as little CO2 per unit of electricity as a wind farm. Moreover, unlike solar panels
and wind turbines, which depend on the weather, a nuclear plant can produce electricity
day and night, in every season. This constant output helps to keep the electricity grid
stable when demand is high. As a result, many experts see nuclear power as a useful complement to wind and solar energy.

However, critics point out that nuclear plants are very expensive and slow to build. Many
projects in Europe and North America have taken more than a decade to finish and have
cost several times the original budget, while wind and solar farms can be installed in a
few years, and emissions must fall quickly during this decade. A second problem is the waste. Spent fuel remains radioactive for thousands
of years, and most countries still have no permanent place to store it. For many
citizens, the possibility of leaving this burden to future generations is hard to accept.

It is very important that you write a balanced argument before presenting your own
point of view. Remember that points of view have to be accounted for by clear and
convincing evidence. Write four paragraphs: an introduction (without your opinion), the
arguments in favour, the arguments against, and a conclusion.
