### Can the right to repair solve our electronic waste problem?

*By Marina Castell, environmental engineer*

**A** Every year the world throws away tens of millions of tonnes of electronic
equipment: phones, laptops, televisions and kitchen appliances. According to estimates
by the United Nations, the total reached about 62 million tonnes in 2022, and it keeps
growing. Only about one fifth of that waste was documented as properly collected and
recycled. The rest was buried, burned or simply forgotten in drawers and warehouses.
Behind these figures lies a simple fact: we are producing electronic rubbish faster than
we are learning to deal with it.

**B** Much of this waste is avoidable, because many devices are thrown away long before
they stop working. Critics blame <u>planned obsolescence</u>: the practice of designing
products so that they fail, or can no longer be updated, after a limited period of time.
The methods are well known. Batteries are glued inside the case instead of being clipped
in, so replacing one means risking a broken screen. Manufacturers use special screws that
ordinary screwdrivers cannot turn, and they refuse to sell spare parts or to publish repair
manuals. Some even use software that rejects a component if it was not installed by an
authorised technician. Spare parts are often sold at prices close to that of a new
device. Most consumers choose to replace rather than repair.

**C** The right-to-repair movement began as a response. It started with small groups of
volunteers who organised <u>repair cafés</u>, where neighbours bring a broken toaster or
a lamp and learn to fix it themselves. Over time, the idea reached lawmakers. In 2024 the
European Union approved rules that oblige manufacturers to repair products during the
legal guarantee period and to supply spare parts at reasonable prices. Several US states
have passed similar laws for electronics, and campaigners now want the same rules to
apply in every country.

**D** The industry is not convinced. Tomás Keller, spokesman for a manufacturers'
association, argues that repairs by independent workshops are dangerous. "A lithium battery
that is badly handled can catch fire," he says, "and the customer will blame the brand."
Manufacturers also claim that opening their designs to repairers would expose trade
secrets, and that keeping spare parts in stock for years is expensive.

**E** To see how repair works in practice, consider a refurbishment centre in Hamburg that
I visited last spring. When a faulty phone arrives, it is registered, and its data are
backed up and then erased, so that the privacy of the owner is protected. A technician
then runs a diagnostic test to find the faulty component. The damaged part is replaced,
usually with a spare taken from a donor device that cannot be repaired itself. Next, the
phone goes through a quality check lasting about an hour. Finally, it is cleaned, repacked
and sold with a one-year guarantee. Whatever cannot be reused is sent to a recycler, where
metals such as gold and copper are recovered.

**F** The evidence from Hamburg challenges Keller's fears. Over eighteen months the centre
carried out about 1,200 battery replacements. The centre recorded no fires and no injuries,
and its technicians follow strict procedures for handling batteries. Repair has clear
advantages. Repaired phones are sold at about 40 per cent less than new ones, and every
repair keeps valuable metals in use instead of sending them to a landfill. However, it also
has drawbacks. A repair usually takes several days, because parts have to be ordered,
whereas a new device can be bought in minutes. In addition, spare parts for older models
become hard to find after a few years.

**G** So, can the right to repair solve our electronic waste problem? On its own, no. Even
the best repair centre cannot fix devices that were designed to be thrown away, and cheap
new gadgets will remain tempting while repair is slow and expensive. Yet repair is
necessary. It changes the habits of consumers and it creates skilled local jobs. The real
answer lies in a combination: laws that force manufacturers to build durable, repairable
products, repair services that ordinary people can afford, and recycling for whatever cannot
be saved.

### Space junk: a traffic jam above our heads

Since the launch of Sputnik in 1957, humans have put thousands of satellites into orbit.
Most of them stopped working long ago, but they have not disappeared. They circle the Earth
together with used rocket stages and fragments left by explosions and collisions. Space
agencies currently track more than 30,000 objects larger than ten centimetres. In addition,
there are about one million pieces between one and ten centimetres, and an estimated 130
million smaller than one centimetre, which are too small to be followed from the ground.

Size is misleading, because speed is what matters. In low orbit, debris travels at about
28,000 kilometres an hour, so even a fleck of paint can chip the window of a spacecraft.
The real fear is a chain reaction. In 1978, the NASA scientist Donald Kessler warned that,
once the density of objects passes a certain level, collisions will create fragments that
cause further collisions, until some orbits become unusable. This scenario is known as the
Kessler syndrome. A first warning came in 2009, when an American communications satellite
collided with a Russian military satellite that was no longer working. The accident produced
thousands of new fragments.

Prevention is the first answer. Regulators are tightening the rules: in the United States,
operators of satellites in low orbit must now remove them within five years of the end of
their mission, instead of the twenty-five years that were previously accepted. Removing
existing debris is harder and more expensive. Engineers have tested nets, harpoons and
robotic arms, and a European mission plans to capture a dead satellite and pull it down
so that it burns up in the atmosphere. Most experts agree that taking out a few of the
largest objects every year would reduce the risk more than anything else, because a single
big collision can create thousands of new fragments. Smaller pieces, in contrast, are
almost impossible to collect.
