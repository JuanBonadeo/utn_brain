### Can hospitals ever be safe from ransomware?

*By Dr Samuel Okoye, cybersecurity researcher*

**A** In 2017, a ransomware program called WannaCry locked the computers of dozens of
hospital trusts in England. Thousands of appointments and operations were cancelled, and
some ambulances were sent to other hospitals. Since then, attacks on health services have
become more frequent and more advanced, and nobody expects them to disappear. The question
is no longer whether a hospital will be attacked, but when, and how well it will cope. Hospitals are not the only victims, but when they are hit the consequences are measured in human health, not only in money.

**B** <u>Ransomware</u> is a type of malicious software, or malware, that encrypts the files
of its victim and demands payment in exchange for the key needed to unlock them. In recent
years, criminals have added a second threat, known as double extortion: they also steal
sensitive data before encrypting it and threaten to publish it if the ransom is not paid.
In this way, even a hospital with perfect backups is under pressure.

**C** Hospitals are attractive targets for three reasons. First, they cannot stop working.
A factory can pause production for a day, but a hospital cannot stop treating patients, so
the pressure to pay is enormous. Second, much of their equipment is old. Medical equipment
is built to last for decades, and many scanners run on software that no longer receives
security updates. Third, patient records are very valuable: on criminal markets, a medical
file can be worth many times more than a stolen credit card number.

**D** A typical attack unfolds in stages. It usually begins with a phishing email: an
employee opens an attachment that looks like an invoice or a message from a colleague. The
malware installs itself and then spreads silently through the network, sometimes for weeks,
looking for valuable data and for the backup systems. Attackers often choose a weekend or a holiday, when fewer technicians are on duty. Once it has found them, the attackers
copy the data and finally launch the encryption. Files become unreadable, and a message
appears on the screens demanding payment, usually in cryptocurrency.

**E** When the attack is discovered, the hospital goes into emergency mode. IT staff
immediately isolate the infected network so that the malware cannot spread any further.
Clinicians switch to paper records. Ambulances are redirected to neighbouring hospitals,
and non-urgent operations are postponed. Then comes the slow part: security experts check
which systems are clean and restore them, one by one, from offline backups. Full recovery
can take weeks. Meanwhile, managers must decide whether to inform patients and the authorities, because hospitals are legally obliged to report any loss of personal data.

**F** Should a hospital pay? Martin Hale, chief executive of a regional hospital group,
argues that paying is the fastest way to protect patients. Paying, he says, can bring the
service back sooner and may stop criminals from publishing patients' data. However, a survey
of 400 hospitals that suffered attacks suggests that the picture is different. Among those
that paid, only about 60 per cent recovered all their data, and they needed on average as
long to return to normal as those that refused. In addition, around one in three of the
hospitals that paid was attacked again within a year, probably because criminals know who is
willing to pay.

**G** So, can hospitals ever be safe? Not completely. Attackers need to succeed only once,
while defenders must succeed every day. But hospitals can become far more resilient.
Offline backups, which criminals cannot encrypt, make payment unnecessary. Dividing the
network into separate sections stops a single infected computer from paralysing the whole
building. Regular training helps staff to recognise phishing emails, and drills help them
to work without computers for several days. None of these measures is expensive compared with the cost of a long shutdown; the difficulty is that they bring no visible benefit until the day they are needed. My answer, therefore, is that perfect security
is an illusion, but resilience is a realistic goal.

### Medicines by air

In many rural areas, a patient who needs blood, a vaccine or an antivenom may live four
hours from the nearest hospital by road, and in the rainy season the road may not even be
passable. Drones are beginning to change that. A drone flies in a straight line, ignores traffic and floods, and does not need a driver. Since 2016, a commercial service in Rwanda
has delivered blood products to remote clinics, and similar programmes now operate in Ghana
and other countries.

A recent pilot study in a rural district compared deliveries by road with deliveries by
drone. The drones could carry up to two kilograms and had a range of about 70 kilometres.
They reached clinics 55 kilometres away in an average of 30 minutes, while the same journey
by road took more than three hours. The share of medicines spoiled by heat fell from twelve
per cent to under two per cent. Each flight, however, cost about three times more than a
delivery by motorbike. Health workers in the district also reported that they felt more confident because they could ask for an emergency delivery at any time.

Drones are not a perfect solution. Heavy rain and strong wind can ground them, and they can
only carry small packages, so they are best suited to urgent, light and valuable items
rather than routine supplies. Costs may fall as the technology matures, but at present the investment is considerable: each distribution centre needs trained operators, a launch system and spare batteries. Regulators also worry about collisions with aircraft, which is
why most flights are restricted to special corridors. In addition, every clinic needs a clear
area where the package can be dropped by parachute. Experts therefore see drones as a
complement to roads, not a replacement: the motorbike for the weekly stock, the drone for the
emergency.

> **The hidden cost of working from home**
>
> Remote work became common among engineers and software developers during the pandemic,
> and many companies have kept it. It offers flexibility, but it also creates difficulties.
> The first is isolation: working alone at home, many developers feel disconnected from their
> team, which can reduce motivation. Some companies organise regular in-person days and
> virtual coffee breaks to rebuild relationships. The second problem is that the boundary
> between work and personal life disappears. Without a commute, people often start earlier
> and finish later, and they are exposed to burnout. Setting fixed hours and respecting the
> workers' right to switch off after them can help. Third, junior engineers learn more
> slowly, because they miss the informal advice that colleagues give in an office. Pairing
> each newcomer with a mentor and promoting pair programming over video calls are possible
> answers. Finally, communication suffers: decisions are made in chat messages that others
> cannot find later. Writing important decisions in shared documents makes information
> accessible and reduces misunderstandings.
