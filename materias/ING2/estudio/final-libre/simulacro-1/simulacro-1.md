Read the following text and answer the questions below it.

### Banning chatbots won't save critical thinking

**A** When ChatGPT was released at the end of 2022, the reaction of many universities
was immediate: ban <u>it</u>. Within weeks, institutions from Australia to France had
blocked access to the tool on campus networks and warned students that using it in
assignments would be treated as plagiarism. The fear was understandable. If a machine
can write a reasonable essay in seconds, how can a lecturer know who actually did the
work?

**B** Three years later, most of those bans have quietly disappeared. <u>This</u> is
not because the original concerns were wrong, but because the bans simply did not work.
Students used the tools on their phones, at home, or through other applications.
Detection software, which promised to identify machine-written text, turned out to be
unreliable: in several cases it accused students who had written every word themselves.

**C** The more serious concern is not cheating but learning itself. Psychologists use
the term "cognitive offloading" to describe what happens when we hand a mental task to
an external tool, the way we stopped memorising phone numbers once our phones could
store them. Offloading is not always bad; it frees our attention for more demanding
work. The danger appears when we offload the very skill we are supposed to be
developing. An engineering student who asks a chatbot to solve every exercise may pass
the course but never learn to reason through a problem.

**D** Recent evidence supports this worry, but with an important nuance. In a study
carried out at a Danish university, two groups of first-year programming students were
given the same assignments. The first group could use an AI assistant freely, while the
second could only use it to explain error messages, not to write code. At the end of
the term, <u>they</u> all took an exam without any assistance. Students in the second
group scored 17 per cent higher on average, even though they had completed their
assignments more slowly.

**E** The lesson seems clear: the problem is not the tool, but the way it is used.
Universities that have redesigned their courses report encouraging results. Some ask
students to submit the conversation they had with the chatbot together with their work,
and to explain which suggestions they rejected and why. Others use oral examinations,
where students must defend their solutions in person. <u>Such strategies</u> make it
pointless to copy an answer, because what is assessed is the reasoning behind it.

**F** Banning chatbots, then, is neither possible nor desirable. Engineers will work
with AI tools throughout their careers, and university is the place to learn to use
them critically. What universities should ban is not the technology, but assessment
methods that only reward the final product. If the process of thinking becomes
visible, the machine stops being a shortcut and becomes a tutor.

### The four-day week arrives in tech

For more than a century, the five-day working week has been the norm in most
industrialised countries. However, a growing number of technology companies are now
experimenting with a radical alternative: working four days a week for the same salary.

The idea is not new, but it gained momentum after the pandemic, when millions of
employees discovered that the traditional office routine was less essential than they
had believed. In 2022, a six-month trial in the United Kingdom involved 61 companies
and around 2,900 workers. At the end of the trial, 56 of the companies decided to
continue with the four-day week, and 18 made the change permanent.

Supporters argue that the benefits go well beyond employee satisfaction. Workers in the
trial reported lower levels of stress and burnout, and the number of sick days fell by
around two thirds. Companies, for their part, found that revenue remained broadly the
same, which suggests that productivity per hour increased. Recruiting also became
easier: firms offering a shorter week received more applications for each vacancy.

Not everyone is convinced. Critics point out that the companies that volunteered for
the trial were probably the ones most likely to succeed, so the results may not apply to
the economy as a whole. Customer-facing businesses, such as hospitals or support
centres, cannot simply close one extra day a week. Moreover, some employees said that
their working days had become more intense, with fewer breaks and less time for
informal conversation with colleagues.

For software developers, the debate has a particular twist. Much of their work requires
long periods of deep concentration, which are frequently interrupted by meetings. Many
of the tech firms that adopted the four-day week did so by cutting meetings rather than
hours of real work — a change that, some argue, they should have made anyway.
