### Smart cities: convenience at what cost?

**A** By 2050, almost seven out of ten people on the planet will live in cities,
according to the United Nations. Feeding, moving and protecting such large populations is
one of the great engineering challenges of the century, and many city governments
believe that data is the answer. The "smart city" uses networks of sensors, cameras and
connected devices to collect information in real time and to manage urban services more
efficiently.

**B** The benefits can be impressive. In Barcelona, sensors buried under parking spaces
tell drivers, through a mobile app, where a free space is available. This reduces
traffic and emissions. Smart street lights dim automatically when nobody is around and
brighten when they detect movement, cutting energy consumption by up to 30 per cent. In
Singapore, a digital model of the entire city allows engineers to simulate how a new
building will affect wind, sunlight and traffic before a single brick is laid.

**C** However, every one of these services depends on collecting data about citizens,
and the line between managing a city and watching its inhabitants can be thin. Cameras
installed to monitor traffic can also be used to recognise faces. Data from public
transport cards reveals where people live, work and spend their evenings. Once such
information exists, it can be used for purposes very different from the original ones:
by governments, by private companies, or by criminals if the systems are hacked.

**D** The case of Toronto illustrates these tensions. In 2017, a company owned by
Alphabet, Google's parent group, announced plans to build a high-tech neighbourhood on
the city's waterfront. The project promised heated pavements and robotic deliveries. It
was abandoned in 2020. Officially, the reasons were economic, but by then citizens and
privacy experts had spent three years asking who would own the data collected in public
spaces, and public opposition had grown considerably.

**E** Experts agree that the solution is not to abandon technology but to govern it
better. Some cities now anonymise the data collected in public spaces. They want to
ensure that it cannot be linked to specific individuals. Others publish online registers
listing every sensor installed in the city and explaining what it measures. Amsterdam
and Helsinki, for example, have created registers of the algorithms the city uses to make
decisions, so that citizens can understand and challenge them.

**F** In the end, a smart city should be judged not by the number of its sensors but by
the quality of life of its inhabitants. Technology can make cities cleaner, safer and
more efficient. It needs the trust of citizens. And that trust, unlike a sensor, cannot
simply be installed.
